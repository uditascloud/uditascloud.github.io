How we cut BigQuery reporting costs by 60%

We didn't buy anything or rewrite the reports. We redesigned the warehouse around how people actually query it.

−60%reporting cost
~$4Msaved per year
3×faster p99 queries

The problem: paying to scan data nobody asked for

BigQuery's on-demand pricing charges by the bytes a query scans, not the bytes it returns. Our reporting warehouse at PayPal had grown table by table, and many large tables were laid out in ways that had nothing to do with how dashboards and analysts read them.

The result was predictable. A report asking for one week of data for one slice of the business could still scan a huge share of a table. Multiply that by every scheduled report, every dashboard refresh and every ad-hoc query, and the bill grows faster than the data does. Latency followed the same pattern: the slowest queries were the ones scanning the most.

Step 1: find out how the tables are really queried

Before changing any table, we needed evidence rather than assumptions. BigQuery records every job, including how many bytes it billed, so the first step was simply to look at where the money was going.

-- Which tables cost the most to query over the last 30 days?
SELECT
  ref.dataset_id,
  ref.table_id,
  COUNT(*)                                  AS queries,
  ROUND(SUM(total_bytes_billed) / POW(1024, 4), 1) AS tib_billed
FROM `region-us`.INFORMATION_SCHEMA.JOBS,
     UNNEST(referenced_tables) AS ref
WHERE creation_time > TIMESTAMP_SUB(CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(), INTERVAL 30 DAY)
  AND job_type = 'QUERY'
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY tib_billed DESC
LIMIT 20;

Illustrative query. A small number of tables usually account for most of the spend.

For the expensive tables, we then read the queries themselves and noted two things: which column people filtered on to pick a time window, and which columns appeared most often in WHERE clauses and joins. Those two answers became the design.

Step 2: partition on the time column queries actually use

Partitioning splits a table into separate segments, typically by date. When a query filters on the partitioning column, BigQuery skips every partition outside the range and you aren't billed for them.

The key word is actually. A table partitioned on ingestion time doesn't help a report that filters on business date. We picked the date column that the real queries filtered on, and we required a partition filter on the biggest tables so that an accidental full-table scan fails fast instead of quietly costing money.

Step 3: cluster on the columns people filter and join by

Within each partition, clustering sorts the data by up to four columns. Queries that filter on those columns read only the relevant blocks. We chose clustering columns from the same query evidence, ordered from the most commonly filtered column to the least.

CREATE TABLE reporting.transactions_v2
PARTITION BY event_date
CLUSTER BY merchant_id, product_line, region
OPTIONS (require_partition_filter = TRUE)
AS SELECT * FROM reporting.transactions;

Illustrative schema. The real column choices came from the query analysis in step 1.

The principle: partitioning and clustering aren't settings you pick once from a checklist. They are a bet on how the table will be read, so make that bet with data.

Step 4: migrate safely and measure

The new layouts were built alongside the existing tables, validated against them, and switched over without changing what report users saw. Afterwards we compared the same workloads before and after on two numbers that matter to different people: bytes billed, which finance cares about, and p99 query latency, which everyone waiting on a dashboard cares about.

Reporting costs dropped by 60%, roughly $4M a year, and p99 latency improved about 3×. Same data, same reports, far less scanning.

What I'd tell anyone doing this

  1. Start from the jobs history, not the schema. The cost problem lives in the queries.
  2. Partition on the column queries filter by, not the one that was convenient at load time.
  3. Make full scans fail loudly on large tables with a required partition filter.
  4. Revisit the design when query patterns change. A good layout for last year's dashboards can be a poor one today.
